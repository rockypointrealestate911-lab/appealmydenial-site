Letter of medical necessity: what your doctor should include

Reviewed November 2, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: A letter of medical necessity is your doctor's written explanation of why you need a service, drug or device. The strongest letters quote the plan's reason for denial and answer it point by point, show how you meet the plan's written criteria, and point to records made at the time of your care. A letter on its own isn't always enough: send the office notes, test results and treatment history that back it up.

When you need one

You'll usually need a letter of medical necessity when a plan:

  • Denies a service as not medically necessary.
  • Requires prior authorization and wants proof first.
  • Won't cover a drug that's off its formulary, or wants you to try other drugs first. See prescription drug denials.
  • Says a treatment is experimental or investigational.

What “medically necessary” means

The letter has to speak the plan's language. A few official definitions:

  • HealthCare.gov: “Health care services or supplies needed to diagnose or treat an illness, injury, condition, disease or its symptoms and that meet accepted standards of medicine.”
  • Medicare.gov: “Services or supplies that meet accepted standards of medical practice to diagnose or treat your medical condition.”
  • Medicare law bars payment for items and services that are not “reasonable and necessary for the diagnosis or treatment of illness or injury or to improve the functioning of a malformed body member.”

Your plan's own definition is in your plan documents, and its detailed rules for a specific service are in its clinical or medical policy. Ask for both. Job-based plans must give you, free on request, the documents and other information relevant to your claim.

Why the records matter as much as the letter

Medicare's rules for its own claim reviewers show why a letter needs backup. CMS's Program Integrity Manual says physician attestations and supplier-prepared statements “by themselves do NOT provide sufficient documentation of medical necessity,” and that what's submitted must match what's in the patient's medical record. Services are expected to be documented in the medical record at the time they're provided, and any later amendment or late entry must show its date and author. CMS also notes that check-box templates often fail to capture enough clinical detail.

Those are Medicare fee-for-service rules, and private plans set their own. But the lesson carries over: a letter is strongest when it summarizes and points to records that already exist, not when it stands alone.

The checklist: what the letter should include

Give this list to your doctor's office:

  1. Who and what. Your name, date of birth, member ID, claim or reference number, and the exact service, drug (with dose) or device, with its code if known.
  2. The denial, quoted. The plan's exact reason, and the name or number of any policy it cited.
  3. Your diagnosis and how it affects your daily life and function.
  4. Your history: treatments already tried, with dates, how long, and what happened (didn't work, side effects, or not safe for you).
  5. How you meet the plan's criteria, item by item, with the record that shows each one.
  6. Why this, and why now: what's expected to improve, and what's likely to happen if treatment is delayed or denied.
  7. Why the alternatives won't do, if the plan suggested a cheaper or different option.
  8. Evidence: medical guidelines or published studies that support the treatment for your condition.
  9. Urgency: if waiting could seriously harm your health, say so plainly and ask for an expedited decision.
  10. Signature and credentials, dated, on letterhead, with a phone number for questions.
  11. Enclosures: a list of the records attached, such as office notes, test and imaging results, and medication history.

Prescription drugs: what the prescriber has to say

For Medicare drug plans (Part D), an exception request needs a supporting statement from the prescriber, by phone or in writing. For a drug that's not on the formulary, the statement must say the drug is medically necessary and explain, for example, that the formulary alternatives would not be as effective for you, would have adverse effects for you, or both; or that other drugs required first have been or are likely to be ineffective or harmful. For a lower copay tier, the statement must say the preferred drugs would not be as effective for you or would have adverse effects, or both. A plan may ask for a written follow-up and more medical documentation if the statement was given by phone.

The clock matters: a Part D plan generally has to decide a standard exception request no later than 72 hours after it gets the prescriber's supporting statement, or 24 hours for an expedited request. If the statement hasn't arrived within 14 days of the request, the clock starts anyway, but the plan may be deciding without your prescriber's input, so get the statement in quickly.

For Marketplace and other plans that must cover essential health benefits, you, someone you designate, or your prescribing doctor can ask for an exception for a drug that's not on the list. The plan must decide within 72 hours, or within 24 hours in exigent circumstances, such as when your health, life or ability to regain maximum function may be seriously at risk, or when you're already being treated with a drug that's not on the list. A denial can go to an independent reviewer.

Who reads it

  • Job-based plans: on appeal, the plan must consider everything you submit, whether or not it was part of the first decision. For denials based on medical judgment, it must consult a health care professional with appropriate training and experience in that field.
  • Medicare Advantage: a denial based on medical necessity must be reviewed by a physician or other appropriate health care professional with expertise suited to the service, and the appeal (reconsideration) must be decided by someone who wasn't involved in the first decision. Plans must make medical necessity decisions based on factors including your medical history and your doctor's recommendations, and they must follow Original Medicare's national and local coverage rules. A plan can use its own internal criteria only when Medicare's criteria aren't fully established, and those criteria must be publicly accessible and based on current evidence. Ask which criteria were used, and have your doctor answer them.
  • External review: in the federal process, the independent reviewer looks at the claim fresh and isn't bound by the plan's decision. It considers your medical records, the attending health care professional's recommendation, practice guidelines that include evidence-based standards, and the plan's clinical review criteria, among other things. Your doctor's recommendation is one of the things the reviewer must consider, so a clear letter matters. How external review works.

How to ask your doctor's office

Offices write many of these, so make it easy:

  • Send a copy of the denial, with the reason highlighted.
  • Send the plan's clinical policy or criteria, if you have it.
  • Give your deadline and the date you need the letter, about two weeks earlier. Work out your deadline.
  • Ask whether the doctor can also request a peer-to-peer conversation with the plan's reviewer, if the plan offers one.
  • Ask for a copy of the letter and the records sent, for your file.

If your doctor's office sends the letter directly to the plan, confirm it was received and attached to your appeal.

A note on medical equipment in Medicare

Medicare stopped using Certificates of Medical Necessity and DME Information Forms for medical equipment claims with dates of service on or after January 1, 2023, after stakeholders said the forms were burdensome and duplicative of information already on the claim or in the medical record. If a supplier asks you for one of these older forms, the medical record is what counts now.

Common questions

What is a letter of medical necessity?

A letter from your doctor explaining why a service, drug or device is needed for your condition. The best ones quote the plan's reason for denial, answer it point by point, and point to your medical records.

Is a doctor's letter enough to win an appeal?

Not always. CMS tells Medicare reviewers that physician attestations by themselves don't provide sufficient documentation of medical necessity; the medical record has to support them. Send office notes, test results and treatment history with the letter.

What does a prescriber need to say for a Medicare Part D exception?

For a non-formulary drug, the prescriber's supporting statement must say the drug is medically necessary, for example because formulary alternatives would not be as effective for you, would have adverse effects, or both. The plan generally decides within 72 hours of getting the statement, or 24 hours if expedited.
