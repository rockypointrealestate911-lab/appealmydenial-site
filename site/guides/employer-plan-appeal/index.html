How to appeal a denial from a health plan through your job

Reviewed October 4, 2026 · 6 min read

Short answer: You have 180 days from receiving the denial to file an appeal with the plan. If it still says no, most plans then offer an independent external review. Your denial letter says which review process applies.

Step 1: The plan's internal appeal

Federal rules give you at least 180 days after you receive a denial to appeal. Check your date. The plan must decide:

  • Urgent care: within 72 hours.
  • Service not yet received: within 30 days (15 days per level if the plan has two levels).
  • Service already received: within 60 days (30 days per level if two levels).

You're entitled to free copies of the documents and records relevant to your claim, including the plan provisions and any clinical criteria used.

Step 2: External review, and which kind

Which external review you get depends on how the plan is funded:

  • Fully insured plans (the employer buys coverage from an insurance company) usually use your state's external review program.
  • Self-funded plans (common at large employers) usually use an accredited independent reviewer that the plan hires under federal rules. Plans run by government employers may use the federal HHS process instead.

Your final denial letter must explain the external review option and deadline. If you aren't sure, ask your HR or benefits office whether the plan is "fully insured" or "self-funded."

Plans that were in place before March 23, 2010 and haven't changed much ("grandfathered" plans) may not have to offer external review.

What to send

  • A short letter that quotes the reason and answers it.
  • For coverage disputes, the page of the Summary Plan Description (SPD) that supports you. How to answer a "not covered" denial.
  • For medical necessity, your doctor's letter and records.

Common questions

How long do I have to appeal an employer health plan denial?

At least 180 days from receiving the denial. Check your letter, and use the earlier date if it states a shorter one.

What's the difference between fully insured and self-funded?

A fully insured plan is bought from an insurance company and is regulated by your state. A self-funded plan pays claims from the employer's own funds and follows federal rules; its external review is usually done by an independent reviewer the plan hires.
