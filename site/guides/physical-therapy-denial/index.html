Physical therapy denied or capped

Reviewed November 9, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: If you have Medicare, there's no hard cap on medically necessary outpatient physical therapy. The old cap is now a dollar threshold ($2,480 for physical and speech therapy combined in 2026) above which your therapist must add a code to the claim. Therapy to maintain function or slow decline can be covered too, so “you've stopped improving” isn't a reason on its own. With other plans, check whether you hit a written visit limit or were denied as not medically necessary. The fix is different for each.

First, find out which kind of denial you have

Read the denial letter or Explanation of Benefits closely. Physical therapy denials usually come down to one of these questions:

  • Is it medically necessary? The plan says the therapy isn't needed, or isn't needed at this frequency. See not medically necessary denials.
  • Are you still improving? The plan says you've “plateaued.” In Medicare, that alone isn't a valid reason (see below).
  • Did you use up a benefit limit? Your plan covers a set number of visits a year and says you've reached it. That's a coverage question, not a medical one. See “not covered” denials.
  • Was approval missing? The plan required prior authorization for more visits. See prior authorization denials.

Original Medicare: no cap, but a threshold

Medicare Part B helps pay for medically necessary outpatient physical therapy if you're eligible, and a doctor or other qualified provider must certify that you need it. After the Part B deductible, you pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount. Medicare.gov says: “There's no limit on how much Medicare pays for your medically necessary outpatient therapy services in one calendar year.”

Medicare used to cap therapy. The Bipartisan Budget Act of 2018 repealed the caps, but the law kept the old cap amounts as thresholds. Above them, claims must include a code called the KX modifier, and CMS says claims over the threshold without it are denied. The amounts for 2026 are:

  • $2,480 for physical therapy and speech-language pathology combined.
  • $2,480 for occupational therapy, counted separately.

Above $3,000 in a year (counted separately for occupational therapy), claims can be picked for targeted medical review. Neither number is a limit on your care. If a claim above the threshold was denied, ask your therapist's billing office whether the KX modifier was left off, and whether they can correct the claim. How billing errors get fixed.

“You've stopped improving” isn't enough

Under the Jimmo v. Sebelius settlement, approved by a court on January 24, 2013, Medicare coverage of skilled care “does not turn on the presence or absence of a beneficiary's potential for improvement, but rather on the beneficiary's need for skilled care.” It applies to skilled nursing facility, home health and outpatient therapy settings. A maintenance program can be covered “so long as the beneficiary requires skilled care for the safe and effective performance of the program.” Medicare.gov says you can get therapy “to help improve or maintain your current function, or slow your rate of decline.”

If your denial says you've plateaued, your appeal should say why you still need a skilled therapist, not just exercises you could do on your own. For example, the therapy may need a therapist's skill to be done safely, or you'd likely decline without it.

If your therapist gives you an ABN

If your therapist thinks Medicare won't pay, they may give you an Advance Beneficiary Notice of Noncoverage (ABN). Medicare's therapy booklet says your provider must tell you your services aren't medically necessary before giving you one, and they “can't give you an ABN only because your therapy expenses have reached a certain amount.” An ABN isn't an official Medicare denial. If you want the therapy and want to keep your right to appeal, choose the option that has the claim sent to Medicare. If you choose not to have it billed, you can't file an appeal.

Medicare Advantage

Medicare Advantage plans may require prior authorization before they cover certain services. But they must follow Original Medicare's national and local coverage rules, including its general coverage conditions. A plan can use its own coverage criteria only when Medicare's criteria aren't fully established, and those criteria must be publicly accessible and based on current evidence. If your plan cut off therapy, ask which criteria it used and whether they're stricter than Original Medicare's. How to appeal in Medicare Advantage.

Marketplace and individual plans

“Rehabilitative and habilitative services and devices” are one of the essential health benefits that Marketplace plans must cover. HealthCare.gov describes them as services and devices that help people with injuries, disabilities or chronic conditions gain or recover mental and physical skills. Federal rules define habilitative services as care that helps a person keep, learn or improve skills and functioning for daily living, and physical therapy can be one of them.

Plans can set visit limits, but since the 2017 plan year, CMS has required separate visit limits for habilitative and rehabilitative services. Federal rules also bar plans from putting less favorable limits on habilitative services than on rehabilitative ones. If you were told you hit your limit, check which limit was counted. Habilitative visits shouldn't use up your rehabilitative visits, or the other way around. How to appeal a Marketplace plan.

Job-based plans

Your plan's visit limits and rules are in its plan documents. You have the right to get, free on request, the documents and other information relevant to your claim. If the denial was a medical judgment, such as “not medically necessary,” the plan must consult a health care professional with appropriate training and experience in that field when it decides your appeal. How to appeal a job-based plan.

Medicaid

For adults, physical therapy is an optional Medicaid benefit, so what's covered depends on your state. For children and young adults under 21, Medicaid's EPSDT benefit covers medically necessary services to “correct and ameliorate health conditions,” even if the state plan doesn't otherwise cover them. If your child's therapy was denied, say in the appeal that EPSDT applies. How to appeal in Medicaid.

How to build the appeal

  1. Check your deadline first. Work out your appeal deadline.
  2. Get the plan's reason and its criteria. Ask which clinical policy or guideline it applied.
  3. Get your therapy records: the evaluation, the plan of care, progress notes, and any test scores or measurements over time.
  4. Ask your therapist and doctor for a letter that explains your goals, what skilled care you still need and why, and what's likely to happen without it. What the letter should include.
  5. Answer the exact reason. For a plateau denial, cite maintenance coverage. For a visit limit, check how the visits were counted. For a missing code, ask for a corrected claim.
  6. If the plan says no again, ask about independent external review. Find your state's rules and contacts.

Keep going to therapy only if you understand what you might owe. Ask the plan and the clinic before you continue.

Common questions

Does Medicare limit how many physical therapy visits I can get?

No. Medicare.gov says there's no limit on how much Medicare pays for medically necessary outpatient therapy. The old cap amount is now a threshold ($2,480 for physical and speech therapy combined in 2026) above which claims must carry the KX modifier.

Can Medicare stop paying for therapy because I'm not improving?

Not for that reason alone. Under the Jimmo settlement, coverage depends on whether you need skilled care, not on whether you can improve. Therapy to maintain function or slow decline can be covered.

Can a Marketplace plan limit physical therapy visits?

Plans can set visit limits, but since 2017 they must keep separate limits for habilitative and rehabilitative services, and habilitative limits can't be less favorable than rehabilitative ones.
