Denied because of a coding or billing error

Reviewed November 2, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: If the denial points to a billing or coding mistake (codes like 16, 4, 11 or 181), the fix usually belongs to the provider's billing office: they correct the claim and send it again. Get an itemized bill, compare it to your Explanation of Benefits, and call the billing office with the exact code and what looks wrong. In Original Medicare, small clerical mistakes are fixed through a reopening, not an appeal. Keep your appeal deadline in view in case the mistake turns out to be something else.

How to tell it's a billing problem

Your Explanation of Benefits (EOB) or the provider's statement may show a two-letter group code and a number, such as CO-16. The number is a standard Claim Adjustment Reason Code. These point to a mistake on the claim rather than a judgment about your care:

  • 16: “Claim/service lacks information or has submission/billing error(s).”
  • 4: “The procedure code is inconsistent with the modifier used.”
  • 11: “The diagnosis is inconsistent with the procedure.”
  • 146: “Diagnosis was invalid for the date(s) of service reported.”
  • 181: “Procedure code was invalid on the date of service.”
  • 182: “Procedure modifier was invalid on the date of service.”
  • 18: an exact duplicate claim or service. If you got the service once and it was billed twice, the duplicate denial is correct, but make sure the original was paid.
  • 109: the claim went to the wrong payer or contractor and has to be sent to the right one.

Some statements also show a remark code that adds detail. For example, M51 means a missing, incomplete or invalid procedure code, M76 a missing, incomplete or invalid diagnosis, and N56 a procedure code that isn't correct or valid for the services or date billed. If instead the denial says the care wasn't needed, wasn't covered, or needed approval first, that's a different problem with a different fix: see not medically necessary, not covered or prior authorization.

Step 1: Get the itemized bill and line it up with your EOB

CMS suggests these checks when you think a medical bill is wrong:

  • Ask for a list of the costs for each item or service you're being billed for.
  • Make sure the “your share” amount on the EOB matches what's on your bill.
  • Make sure you weren't billed twice for the same service.
  • Check whether the billing codes match the care you got. If they don't, contact the provider's billing department.

Look for the simple things too: your name, date of birth and member ID, the date of service, the right insurance company, and the provider's name. A wrong digit in an ID or a date can be enough to stop a claim.

Step 2: Call the provider's billing office

The provider submitted the claim, so the provider is usually the one who can correct and resubmit it. When you call:

  1. Give the date of service, the claim number from your EOB, and the exact reason code and wording.
  2. Say what looks wrong, for example: “The EOB says the diagnosis doesn't match the procedure. Can you check the codes and send a corrected claim?”
  3. Ask them to hold the account while the claim is corrected, so it isn't sent to you as unpaid.
  4. Write down the date, the person's name, and what they promised. Call back in two to three weeks and check your next EOB.

Step 3: Ask your plan to help

CMS notes that your insurance company may be able to fix billing errors with your provider. Call the member services number on your card, explain what you found, and ask whether the plan can reprocess the claim once it has the corrected information. If the EOB doesn't match the provider's bill, CMS's advice is to contact the provider. If the EOB and the bill match but you still think the plan got it wrong, you can ask your insurance company to reconsider its decision (an internal appeal).

Original Medicare: reopenings, not appeals, for clerical errors

Medicare has its own process for small mistakes. Under federal rules, Medicare contractors must treat clerical errors, including minor errors and omissions, as reopenings rather than appeals. A clerical error includes human or mechanical errors by the person who filed or by the contractor. CMS's examples include transposed procedure or diagnosis codes, and incorrect data such as a provider number, modifier or date of service.

  • Time limit: a reopening can be requested within 1 year of the initial determination for any reason, or within 4 years for good cause.
  • How: CMS's manual says minor errors can be handled by phone or in writing. When a lot of documentation is needed, a written request or an appeal is the better route.
  • If Medicare won't reopen: a contractor's choice not to reopen can't be appealed, but you can still appeal the original decision through a redetermination. That deadline is 120 days from when you receive the notice. How Original Medicare appeals work.

“Unprocessable” claims are different. If a Medicare claim came back with remark code MA130, it was returned because it had incomplete or invalid information. No appeal rights apply. The remark code itself tells the provider to “submit a new claim with the complete/correct information.” Medicare's normal 12-month claim filing limit still applies, so ask the provider not to wait. Your right to an itemized statement in Medicare

Federal law gives people with Medicare the right to ask, in writing, any doctor, provider or supplier for an itemized statement of items and services Medicare paid for. They must send it within 30 days, and a provider who knowingly fails to can face a civil penalty of up to $100 for each failure. Within 90 days of getting the statement, you can ask Medicare to review it for items that weren't provided or other billing irregularities, such as duplicate charges.

Medicare's guide to the Medicare Summary Notice (MSN) suggests checking each listed service against your receipts and bills. If something doesn't match, ask the provider to contact Medicare's claims office to correct it. If a claim was denied, you can call or write the provider for an itemized statement, and call 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227) with questions about a decision on the notice. Free one-on-one help is also available from your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP).

Job-based plans: they must say what's missing

For job-based plans, the denial notice must describe any additional material or information needed to complete the claim, and explain why it's needed. Use that sentence as the to-do list for the billing office. If the plan instead extends its decision time because information is missing, the notice must describe the information it needs, and for most claims (not urgent care) you get at least 45 days to provide it.

When a “billing error” is really something else

Sometimes a corrected claim is denied again for a different reason, or the billing office says the claim was right. Then you're looking at a regular denial. Read the new reason, and if you disagree, file a written appeal before your deadline. Correcting a claim doesn't always stop the appeal clock, so note the date on the original denial. Common questions

What does denial code CO-16 mean?

CO-16 means the claim or service lacks information or has a submission or billing error. CO stands for “Contractual Obligation.” Usually the provider's billing office has to correct the claim and resubmit it.

Can I appeal a Medicare claim that was denied for a coding mistake?

In Original Medicare, clerical errors such as transposed codes or a wrong date of service are fixed through a reopening, not an appeal. A reopening can be requested within 1 year for any reason. If the claim was returned as unprocessable (remark code MA130), there are no appeal rights; the provider must submit a new, corrected claim.

How do I get an itemized bill?

Ask the provider's billing office. If you have Medicare, federal law lets you request, in writing, an itemized statement of items and services Medicare paid for, and the provider must send it within 30 days. For a denied claim, Medicare's notice suggests calling or writing the provider for an itemized statement.
