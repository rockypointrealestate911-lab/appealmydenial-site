Home health care denied

Reviewed November 9, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: Medicare covers home health care at no cost to you if you're homebound, need part-time or intermittent skilled care, are under a doctor's or allowed practitioner's care with a plan of care, and use a Medicare-certified agency. Being homebound doesn't mean you never leave: trips for medical care, religious services and short, infrequent outings are allowed. If your agency says covered care is ending, you can ask for a fast appeal. Ask no later than noon the day before the end date on your notice.

Who qualifies in Original Medicare

To get Medicare home health coverage, you must meet every one of these:

  • You're confined to the home (“homebound”; see below).
  • You're under the care of a physician or allowed practitioner, and you're under a plan of care.
  • You need skilled care: intermittent skilled nursing, physical therapy, speech-language pathology, or continuing occupational therapy.
  • A Medicare-certified home health agency provides the care.

Medicare.gov adds that a doctor or other health care provider, like a nurse practitioner, must see you in person, and must order your care.

What you pay: “You pay nothing for covered home health services.” For Medicare-covered medical equipment, you pay 20% of the Medicare-approved amount after the Part B deductible. Medicare doesn't pay for 24-hour-a-day care at home.

What “homebound” means

Medicare.gov describes it this way: leaving home isn't recommended because of your condition, or you have trouble leaving home without help. And you're normally unable to leave home, and leaving takes a lot of effort.

You can still leave sometimes. “You may leave home for medical treatment or short, infrequent absences for non-medical reasons, like attending religious services.” Medicare's manual for home health says:

  • Trips for health care treatment don't count against you.
  • Attending adult day care doesn't disqualify you.
  • Any absence to attend a religious service is treated as infrequent or short.
  • Examples of other short or infrequent absences include “an occasional trip to the barber, a walk around the block or a drive,” or going to a family reunion, funeral or graduation.

If your denial says you aren't homebound, your appeal should describe, with your doctor's support, what it takes for you to leave home and how often you do.

What “part-time or intermittent” means

Medicare home health is not round-the-clock care. Under the Medicare law:

  • Intermittent skilled nursing means care that's provided or needed on fewer than 7 days each week, or less than 8 hours each day for periods of 21 days or less (with extensions in exceptional circumstances when the need is finite and predictable).
  • Part-time or intermittent skilled nursing and home health aide services, combined, must be less than 8 hours each day and 28 or fewer hours each week. Up to 35 hours a week may be allowed, reviewed case by case.

“Not improving” isn't a valid reason

Under the Jimmo v. Sebelius settlement, Medicare coverage of skilled care “does not turn on the presence or absence of a beneficiary's potential for improvement, but rather on the beneficiary's need for skilled care.” It applies to home health, skilled nursing facility and outpatient therapy settings. Skilled care can be covered when it's “necessary to maintain the patient's current condition or prevent or slow further deterioration.” If you're told care is ending because you've stopped improving, ask what skilled need the agency or plan says you no longer have.

If your care is being cut or ended

Home health agencies give written notices before changing your care. Know which one you have:

  • Notice of Medicare Non-Coverage (NOMNC): all covered care is ending. You should get it at least 2 days before covered services end. You can ask the Beneficiary and Family Centered Care Quality Improvement Organization (BFCC-QIO), an independent reviewer, for a fast appeal. Ask no later than noon the day before the end date on the notice. The QIO generally decides by close of business the day after it gets the information it needs. More on fast appeals.
  • Home Health Change of Care Notice (HHCCN): the agency is reducing or stopping some items or services in your plan of care. Agencies must give written notice before reducing or ending an item or service.
  • Advance Beneficiary Notice of Noncoverage (ABN): the agency thinks Medicare won't pay for a service. An ABN isn't an official Medicare denial. If you want the service, choose the option that has the claim sent to Medicare so you can appeal if it's denied.

For a regular denial of a home health claim in Original Medicare, use the standard appeal process. How to appeal in Original Medicare.

A note on the Review Choice Demonstration

In Illinois, Ohio, Texas, North Carolina, Florida and Oklahoma, CMS runs a Review Choice Demonstration that reviews home health agencies' Medicare claims, either before the claim is paid or afterward. CMS has announced plans to add California for billing periods starting on or after January 18, 2027, pending federal paperwork approval. That's a review of the agency's billing, not an appeal step for you. If your agency says a claim review is holding up your care, ask it to explain what's needed and who must provide it.

Medicare Advantage

Medicare Advantage plans must cover all the services Original Medicare Parts A and B cover, and follow Medicare's national and local coverage rules. They can require prior authorization, but CMS says it may only be used to confirm diagnoses or other medical criteria, or to make sure a service is medically necessary, and an approval must stay valid for as long as medically reasonable and necessary. Once a plan approves a service through prior authorization, it may not deny coverage later on the basis of lack of medical necessity, except for good cause or reliable evidence of fraud or similar fault. If you switch to a new Medicare Advantage plan during an active course of treatment, CMS requires most plans (coordinated care plans) to give at least a 90-day transition period, during which they can't require prior authorization for that treatment. How to appeal in Medicare Advantage.

Medicaid

Home health is a mandatory Medicaid benefit. Federal rules say Medicaid can't limit home health to people who are homebound, and coverage can't depend on needing nursing or therapy services. A practitioner must have a face-to-face encounter with you related to the main reason for home health, within the 90 days before or the 30 days after care starts. How to appeal in Medicaid.

Job-based and Marketplace plans

Coverage rules for home health vary by plan. Check your plan documents for what's covered, any visit limits, and whether prior authorization is needed, then appeal as you would any other denial. The general appeal steps.

How to build the appeal

  1. Act fast if care is ending. Call the QIO on your notice by noon the day before the end date. For other denials, work out your deadline.
  2. Get the reason in writing, and the criteria the plan or agency used.
  3. Get your records: the plan of care, visit notes, the in-person visit note, and therapy or nursing assessments.
  4. Ask your doctor for a letter that explains why you're homebound, what skilled care you need, and what's likely to happen without it. What the letter should include.
  5. If the plan says no again, ask about the next level of appeal or external review. Find your state's contacts.

Common questions

Can I leave the house and still be homebound for Medicare?

Yes. You can leave for medical treatment and for short, infrequent non-medical absences, like religious services. Medicare's manual also says adult day care doesn't disqualify you, and gives examples like an occasional trip to the barber or a walk around the block.

How do I appeal when my home health care is ending?

If you have Original Medicare and got a Notice of Medicare Non-Coverage, ask the BFCC-QIO on the notice for a fast appeal no later than noon the day before the end date.

Does Medicare cover home health if I'm not getting better?

It can. Under the Jimmo settlement, coverage depends on your need for skilled care, not on whether you can improve. Skilled care to maintain your condition or slow decline can be covered.
