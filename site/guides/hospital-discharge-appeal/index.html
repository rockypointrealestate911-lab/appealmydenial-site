Being discharged from the hospital too soon

Reviewed November 16, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: If you have Medicare and think you're being discharged from the hospital too soon, call the BFCC-QIO listed on your Important Message from Medicare no later than the day you're scheduled to leave. If you ask on time, you can stay in the hospital while the QIO reviews your case, and you won't pay for that time except your usual coinsurance or deductibles. The QIO decides within one day of getting the information it needs.

The notice that starts the clock

Within 2 days of your admission as an inpatient, the hospital should give you a notice called “An Important Message from Medicare about Your Rights” (often called the IM).

You should also get it before you leave. If the hospital gave you the IM more than 2 days before your discharge day, it must give you a copy of your signed IM, or a new one, before you're discharged.

The IM explains your right to a fast appeal and lists the phone number of your Beneficiary and Family Centered Care Quality Improvement Organization (BFCC-QIO). The QIO is an independent reviewer, not the hospital and not your plan. Keep the notice somewhere you can find it.

How to ask for a fast appeal

  1. Find the QIO's number on your IM. If you can't find the notice, ask the nurse, case manager or social worker for another copy.
  2. Call no later than the day you're scheduled to be discharged. Follow the directions on the IM. Don't leave the hospital first.
  3. Explain why you think you're not ready. For example, your symptoms aren't controlled, you can't safely get around, or the care you'll need after discharge isn't arranged.
  4. Watch for the detailed notice. After the QIO tells the hospital (or your plan) about your appeal, the hospital gives you a Detailed Notice of Discharge by noon the next day. It explains why the hospital thinks you're ready to leave.
  5. Talk to your doctor. Ask whether they agree with the discharge and, if not, to tell the QIO.

The QIO decides within one day of getting the information it needs.

What you pay while you wait

If you ask on time, you can stay in the hospital while you wait for the QIO's decision. You won't have to pay for your stay during that time, except for applicable coinsurance or deductibles.

If the QIO decides you're ready to go, you still won't be responsible for hospital charges (other than coinsurance or deductibles) through noon of the day after the QIO gives you its decision. After that, you may have to pay if you stay.

If you miss the deadline, you can still ask, but you might be responsible for the cost of the hospital stay after the original day the hospital planned to discharge you.

For reference, in 2026 the Part A inpatient hospital deductible is $1,736 per benefit period. It covers your share of costs for the first 60 days of Medicare-covered inpatient hospital care in a benefit period.

Are you an inpatient or under observation?

These rights are for hospital inpatients. You're an inpatient starting when you're formally admitted with a doctor's order. Without that order, you're an outpatient, even if you spend the night in the hospital.

Observation services are hospital outpatient services you get while your doctor decides whether to admit you or discharge you. If you're getting outpatient observation services for more than 24 hours, the hospital must give you a Medicare Outpatient Observation Notice (MOON). Medicare.gov says an inpatient admission is generally appropriate when you're expected to need 2 or more midnights of medically necessary hospital care.

Your status matters for two reasons. It changes what you pay, because observation is billed as outpatient services under Part B instead of Part A. And observation time doesn't count toward the 3-day inpatient stay Original Medicare generally requires before it covers skilled nursing facility care. Each day you're in the hospital, you or a caregiver should ask whether you're an inpatient or an outpatient. How the 3-day rule affects nursing facility coverage.

If you were admitted, then switched to observation

If you were admitted as an inpatient and the hospital changed your status to outpatient receiving observation services, you can ask for a fast appeal while you're still in the hospital. Medicare.gov has a separate process for people who weren't able to appeal at the time. The window for filing new after-the-fact appeals (for inpatient admissions from January 1, 2009 through February 13, 2025) ended on January 2, 2026; later requests are denied unless you can show good cause for filing late. If this applies to you, start with Medicare.gov's page on appealing a change in status, or call 1-800-MEDICARE.

Medicare Advantage

If you're in a Medicare Advantage plan, you can ask for an appeal, but Medicare.gov says different rules apply. When a Medicare health plan member asks for a fast review, the BFCC-QIO notifies the plan and the hospital. Medicare.gov ties the hospital protections to an inpatient admission your plan authorized; if the plan never authorized the admission, you may need to appeal the plan's denial of coverage for it to pay. Read any notice the hospital or plan gives you and follow its instructions. How to appeal in Medicare Advantage.

After you leave the hospital

If you're going to a skilled nursing facility, or getting home health care, the same kind of fast appeal is available when that care is ending. You should get a Notice of Medicare Non-Coverage at least 2 days before covered services end; call the QIO on it no later than noon the day before the end date. If a hospital claim is later denied and shows up on your Medicare Summary Notice, use the regular appeal process. In Original Medicare, you ask for a redetermination within 120 days of receiving the notice. How to appeal in Original Medicare. Work out your deadline.

Checklist for the day of discharge

  • Do you have your Important Message from Medicare? Where's the QIO's number?
  • Have you called the QIO today, before leaving? Write down the date, time and who you spoke to.
  • Have you asked whether you're an inpatient or under observation?
  • Do you have the Detailed Notice of Discharge, and does its reason make sense to you?
  • Is the care you'll need after discharge (a nursing facility, home health, equipment, medications) actually arranged?
  • Need help? Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) offers free Medicare counseling. Find your state's contacts.

Common questions

How do I appeal a hospital discharge with Medicare?

Call the BFCC-QIO listed on your Important Message from Medicare no later than the day you're scheduled to be discharged. If you ask on time, you can stay in the hospital while the QIO reviews your case.

Do I have to pay to stay in the hospital during a discharge appeal?

If you ask on time, you won't pay for the stay while you wait, except for applicable coinsurance or deductibles. If the QIO decides you're ready to leave, you aren't responsible for charges (other than coinsurance or deductibles) through noon of the day after its decision.

How fast does the QIO decide a hospital discharge appeal?

Within one day of getting the information it needs.

Does observation status count as being admitted?

No. Observation services are outpatient services. You're an inpatient only once you're formally admitted with a doctor's order, even if you spend the night under observation.
