Ambulance claim denied

Reviewed November 23, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: Medicare Part B covers an ambulance when traveling in any other vehicle could endanger your health, and only to the nearest appropriate facility. Non-emergency rides usually need a written order from your doctor. Most denials say the ride wasn't medically necessary or the paperwork was missing, so ask the ambulance company and your doctor to fix the records, and appeal within 120 days of your Medicare Summary Notice. With private insurance, federal surprise-billing protections cover air ambulances but generally not ground ambulances, though some states have their own rules.

What Medicare covers

Medicare covers and helps pay for ambulance services only when other transportation could endanger your health. Medicare.gov gives examples of emergencies like being in shock, unconscious or bleeding heavily. The main rules:

  • Emergency ground ambulance. Part B covers ground ambulance transportation when traveling in any other vehicle could endanger your health.
  • Nearest appropriate facility. Medicare will only cover ambulance services to the nearest appropriate medical facility. Under the federal rule, that's the nearest facility that can provide the level and type of care you need for your illness or injury. If you choose to go to a facility farther away, Medicare will only pay up to what it would cost to take you to the nearest appropriate facility.
  • Air ambulance. Medicare may pay for emergency transport by airplane or helicopter if you need immediate and rapid transport that ground transportation can't provide.
  • Non-emergency ambulance. In some cases Medicare may cover limited, medically necessary, non-emergency ambulance transportation if your doctor writes an order stating that you need it because of your medical condition.

What you pay: after you meet the Part B deductible, 20% of the Medicare-approved amount.

Why ambulance claims get denied

Look at your Medicare Summary Notice or denial letter for the reason. How to read a denial letter. Common problems:

  • "Not medically necessary." Medicare's rule covers ambulance transport when your medical condition is such that other means of transportation are contraindicated, meaning they could harm you. The ambulance crew's run report and your medical records need to show why a car, taxi or wheelchair van wasn't safe.
  • Missing certification for a non-emergency ride. For many non-emergency trips, the ambulance company needs a signed statement from your doctor (a physician certification statement). For scheduled, repetitive trips, it must be dated no earlier than 60 days before the ride. If the doctor's statement can't be obtained, another professional who has personal knowledge of your condition and works for your doctor or the hospital or facility, such as a nurse practitioner, nurse, social worker, case manager or discharge planner, can sign instead.
  • Not "bed-confined" when that was the reason given. Medicare defines bed-confined as being unable to get up from bed without help, unable to walk, and unable to sit in a chair or wheelchair. All three must apply. Being bed-confined is one factor Medicare considers, but it isn't the only way to qualify.
  • Taken somewhere other than the nearest appropriate facility.
  • Missing prior authorization for repeat trips (see below).

Scheduled non-emergency rides, like dialysis

If you need a scheduled, non-emergency ambulance often, prior authorization may apply. Medicare.gov says this covers trips 3 or more round trips in a 10-day period, or at least once a week for 3 weeks or more, with any ambulance company nationwide. Its example is someone with end-stage renal disease going between home and a dialysis facility.

  • Either you or your ambulance company may request prior authorization. CMS says the ambulance company can bill the first three round trips without it.
  • If the request is denied and you keep using the service, Medicare will deny the claim and the ambulance company may bill you for all charges.
  • If you don't qualify, CMS points to other options: you may be eligible for transportation under Medicaid or PACE (Programs of All-inclusive Care for the Elderly). The Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116) and your State Health Insurance Assistance Program can help you find local rides.

If the ambulance company gives you an ABN

For a non-emergency ride, if the ambulance company believes Medicare may not pay, it must give you an Advance Beneficiary Notice of Noncoverage (ABN) to be able to charge you. An ABN isn't an official Medicare denial. If you want the ride and want to keep your right to appeal, choose the option that has the claim sent to Medicare. If you pick the option where no claim is sent, you can't appeal.

How to appeal in Original Medicare

You must actually get the service, and a claim must be sent to Medicare, before you can appeal. Then:

  1. Find the ambulance claim on your Medicare Summary Notice and note the reason it was denied.
  2. Call the ambulance company. Ask what was missing. If the claim lacked a certification statement or a clear description of your condition, the company may be able to fix it.
  3. Get your records. Ask the ambulance company for the trip (run) report, and ask your doctor or the hospital for records from that day.
  4. Ask your doctor for a short letter explaining why other transportation would have endangered your health, and, for a non-emergency ride, why you needed an ambulance. What a letter of medical necessity should include.
  5. Ask for a redetermination within 120 days of receiving the notice. Work out your deadline · How to appeal in Original Medicare.

If you have a Medicare Advantage plan and it denies an ambulance claim, you can ask the plan to reconsider. How to appeal in Medicare Advantage.

Private insurance: surprise ambulance bills

The federal No Surprises Act protects you, in most cases, from unexpected out-of-network bills for emergency room care, certain non-emergency care at in-network facilities, and air ambulance services.

Ground ambulances are different. CMS says ground ambulance services generally aren't covered by the No Surprises Act's billing protections, unless a state law has different rules, and they're still allowed to charge out-of-network rates. A KFF analysis published in 2021 found that half of emergency ground ambulance rides result in an out-of-network charge for people with private insurance.

A federal advisory committee on ground ambulance billing, created by the No Surprises Act, issued recommendations in 2024. It recommended prohibiting balance billing for out-of-network ground ambulance rides and noted that several states have already acted. Those are recommendations, not federal law. Check with your state insurance department to see whether your state limits ground ambulance bills.

What to do with a private-plan denial or bill

  1. Read the explanation of benefits. Is it a denial (the plan won't pay at all), or a balance bill (the plan paid and the ambulance company is billing you the rest)?
  2. For emergencies, cite the emergency rules. For emergency services, plans covered by federal rules can't require prior authorization and can't charge you higher cost sharing (copays or coinsurance) for out-of-network emergency care than for in-network care. If the plan treated your emergency ride as non-emergency, appeal and include records showing it was an emergency.
  3. Appeal within 180 days. HealthCare.gov says you must file an internal appeal within 180 days of receiving the denial notice. How to appeal a health insurance denial.
  4. Ask for external review if the plan says no again and the denial involves medical judgment. How external review works.
  5. Got a surprise air ambulance bill? Our out-of-network guide explains the No Surprises Act and how to report a bill.

Medicaid

State Medicaid programs must make sure enrollees have necessary transportation to and from providers. CMS says this covers both emergency transportation and non-emergency medical transportation (NEMT). Each state runs its own program, so call your Medicaid plan or state agency to ask how to schedule rides. If your Medicaid plan denies a ride or claim, you can appeal. Common questions

Why did Medicare deny my ambulance ride?

Common reasons are that the records don't show other transportation would have endangered your health, a non-emergency ride had no doctor's order or certification, you were taken somewhere other than the nearest appropriate facility, or repeat scheduled rides lacked prior authorization.

How long do I have to appeal a Medicare ambulance denial?

In Original Medicare, ask for a redetermination within 120 days of receiving your Medicare Summary Notice.

Does the No Surprises Act cover ambulance bills?

It covers air ambulance services. CMS says ground ambulance services generally aren't covered by its billing protections unless a state law has different rules.

Does Medicare cover ambulance rides to dialysis?

Medicare may cover medically necessary non-emergency ambulance rides with a written order from your doctor. If you need rides often, such as 3 or more round trips in 10 days, prior authorization may apply.
