Genetic testing denied

Reviewed November 23, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: Coverage for genetic tests depends on why the test is being done. Most private plans must cover BRCA genetic counseling and, if indicated, testing for women at higher risk with no cost sharing in network. Medicare generally covers a genetic test only when your treating doctor orders it to diagnose or manage a medical problem, not as screening. If you're denied, ask what the plan's policy requires, have your doctor document how the result will change your care, and appeal.

Why genetic tests get denied

The National Human Genome Research Institute notes that insurers may find it hard to evaluate what type of genetic test was performed, whether it was appropriate, and whether it is scientifically valid. Denials often come down to one of these:

  • Screening vs. diagnosis. The plan sees the test as screening someone without symptoms or a qualifying risk, not testing to diagnose or treat.
  • Criteria not met. The plan's policy covers the test only for people with a certain personal or family history, and the records sent don't show it.
  • No genetic counseling first. For BRCA testing, the USPSTF recommends testing, if indicated, after genetic counseling, and one Medicare contractor's policy requires independent counseling before multigene panel tests.
  • Prior authorization wasn't requested or was denied. What to do about a prior authorization denial.
  • "Experimental" or "investigational." The plan says the test isn't proven for your situation. How to appeal an experimental denial.

Your denial letter should say which reason applies. How to read a denial letter.

BRCA counseling and testing under the ACA

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommends that primary care clinicians assess women with a personal or family history of breast, ovarian, tubal or peritoneal cancer, or an ancestry linked to BRCA1/2 gene mutations, with a brief familial risk assessment tool. Women with a positive result should receive genetic counseling and, if indicated after counseling, genetic testing (grade B, 2019; the Task Force is updating this recommendation).

  • HealthCare.gov lists BRCA genetic test counseling for women at higher risk among the preventive services Marketplace plans and many other plans must cover without a copayment or coinsurance. These services are generally covered at no cost when you use an in-network provider.
  • KFF says this BRCA screening is required to be covered by insurance plans without cost sharing.
  • In June 2025 the Supreme Court upheld the ACA's preventive services requirement in Kennedy v. Braidwood. KFF says these services continue to be available without cost sharing to most people with private coverage.
  • Exceptions: grandfathered plans (for example, an individual policy bought on or before March 23, 2010) may not include some ACA protections, including free preventive care.

The USPSTF recommends against routine risk assessment, counseling or testing for women whose personal or family history or ancestry isn't linked to harmful BRCA1/2 mutations. So if you were billed or denied, the question is usually whether your records show the risk factors.

If you were charged a copay or denied: ask the plan to reprocess the claim as a preventive service, and make sure your doctor's records note the family history or risk assessment result. If it still says no, appeal.

Medicare and genetic tests

  • Diagnostic tests. Part B covers medically necessary diagnostic laboratory tests when your provider orders them, and you usually pay nothing for covered lab tests.
  • Your treating doctor must order it. Under the federal rule, a diagnostic test must be ordered by the doctor treating you for a specific medical problem who uses the results to manage that problem. Tests not ordered by your treating doctor are not considered reasonable and necessary.
  • Screening is generally excluded. Medicare excludes exams done for a purpose other than treating or diagnosing a specific illness, symptom, complaint or injury, apart from screenings the law specifically lists. KFF notes BRCA genetic testing isn't required to be covered nationally under Medicare, but may be covered in some regions under local coverage determinations.
  • Tumor and inherited cancer testing. Medicare's national policy on next-generation sequencing (NCD 90.2) covers tumor testing for people with recurrent, relapsed, refractory, metastatic, or advanced stage III or IV cancer when ordered by a treating physician and other requirements are met. It also covers inherited (germline) testing for people with ovarian or breast cancer who have a clinical indication and a risk factor for hereditary breast or ovarian cancer.
  • Regional rules. Medicare contractors issue local policies. For example, one contractor's BRCA policy (L36499) says testing people who don't have the disease, including family members, isn't covered. Ask the lab or your doctor which policy applies to you.

If a lab thinks Medicare won't pay, it may give you an Advance Beneficiary Notice (ABN). An ABN isn't an official denial. If you want the test and want to keep your right to appeal, choose the option that has the claim sent to Medicare.

Medicaid

KFF says women in Medicaid expansion programs are entitled to the same screening and preventive services, including BRCA counseling and testing, as women with private insurance. In traditional Medicaid, the scope of coverage is set by each state. For children and young people under 21, Medicaid's EPSDT benefit requires states to provide follow-up diagnostic testing and evaluation, including services the state doesn't otherwise cover for adults. How to appeal a Medicaid denial.

What GINA does and doesn't do

The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) is about discrimination, not coverage of tests. Under GINA, health insurers may not use genetic information to decide whether you're eligible for insurance or to set premiums, and may not request or require you or your family members to have genetic testing. GINA's health insurance protections don't cover long-term care, life or disability insurance.

How to appeal a genetic testing denial

  1. Get the plan's policy. Ask for the specific medical policy or criteria the plan used, so you can answer each point.
  2. Have your doctor connect the test to your care. A letter should explain your symptoms, diagnosis or family history, which test is being ordered, and how the result will change treatment, surgery, monitoring or medication. What the letter should include.
  3. Include a genetic counseling note if you had counseling, and the family history it documented.
  4. Check for coding problems. If the plan denied it as screening when it was diagnostic, ask the lab and doctor whether the claim was coded correctly. Fixing a coding or billing error.
  5. Appeal on time. Private plans: file within 180 days of the denial notice. Original Medicare: ask for a redetermination within 120 days of your Medicare Summary Notice. Find your deadline.
  6. Ask for external review if a private plan says no again. Denials involving medical judgment, or a finding that a test is experimental or investigational, can go to an independent reviewer. How external review works · Your state's contacts.

Before a test, it's worth calling your plan to ask whether it's covered and whether it needs prior approval. If it's for active cancer treatment and waiting could harm you, ask for an expedited decision.

Common questions

Does insurance have to cover BRCA genetic testing?

Most private plans must cover BRCA genetic counseling, and testing if indicated after counseling, for women at higher risk, with no cost sharing in network. Grandfathered plans may be an exception.

Does Medicare cover genetic testing?

Medicare generally covers a genetic test when your treating doctor orders it to diagnose or manage a medical problem and Medicare's coverage rules are met. Screening people without symptoms is generally excluded.

Does GINA require insurance to pay for genetic tests?

No. GINA bars health insurers from using genetic information for eligibility or premiums and from requiring genetic testing. It's about discrimination, not coverage.

How long do I have to appeal a genetic testing denial?

For most private plans, 180 days from the denial notice. In Original Medicare, 120 days from your Medicare Summary Notice.
