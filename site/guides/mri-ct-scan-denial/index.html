MRI or CT scan denied

Reviewed November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: Most MRI and CT scan denials come from prior authorization: the plan wants proof the scan is needed before it pays. Ask your doctor's office to send the plan your symptoms, exam findings and what's been tried, ask for the specific reason and the criteria used, and appeal. Original Medicare usually doesn't require prior approval. In Medicare Advantage, most appealed prior authorization denials in 2024 were overturned.

Why imaging gets denied

Prior authorization means approval from a health plan that may be required before you get a service, for it to be covered. HealthCare.gov notes that it “isn't a promise your health insurance or plan will cover the cost.” A scan can be denied before it happens (the request is turned down) or after (the claim is denied because approval was missing or the plan decides the scan wasn't needed). If the scan hasn't happened yet, you have time to fix the request. If it already happened, see denied for no prior authorization.

Read the denial closely

Before you call anyone, find these on the letter or Explanation of Benefits:

  • The exact reason. Was approval never requested, was the scan judged not medically necessary, or did the plan want a different test first?
  • Who decided, and the phone number or address for questions and appeals.
  • Your deadline to appeal, and whether you can ask for a faster decision.
  • The policy or criteria the plan named, if any.

Write these down with the date. They shape everything else you do. How to read a denial letter.

Original Medicare

Medicare Part B covers diagnostic tests like CT scans and MRIs “when your provider orders them to find or treat a medical problem.” Medicare.gov says that in most cases, you don't need approval (prior authorization) for Original Medicare to cover your services or supplies.

What you pay: after the Part B deductible, 20% of the Medicare-approved amount in a doctor's office or independent testing facility. At a hospital outpatient department, you also pay the hospital a copayment that may be more than 20% of the approved amount; in most cases, it can't be more than the Part A hospital stay deductible.

Accreditation: Medicare only pays for these scans outside a hospital if the provider is accredited. If Medicare doesn't pay because the provider isn't accredited, the provider can't bill you for the test.

The imaging “AUC” program is gone. Medicare had planned to require doctors to consult appropriate use criteria before ordering advanced imaging. CMS paused the program and rescinded its regulations effective January 1, 2024.

If the imaging center thinks Medicare won't pay, it may give you an Advance Beneficiary Notice (ABN). If you want the scan and want to keep your right to appeal, choose the option that has the claim sent to Medicare. How to appeal in Original Medicare.

Medicare Advantage

Medicare Advantage plans often require prior authorization. In 2024, Medicare Advantage insurers got nearly 53 million prior authorization requests and denied 4.1 million (7.7%) in full or in part. Just 11.5% of denials were appealed, but 80.7% of appeals were partly or fully overturned.

Your rights:

  • The plan must follow Original Medicare's national and local coverage rules. It can use its own criteria only when Medicare's aren't fully established, and they must be publicly accessible.
  • Once a plan approves a scan through prior authorization, it may not later deny coverage on the basis of lack of medical necessity, except in limited cases such as fraud.

How to appeal in Medicare Advantage.

New prior authorization rules for 2026

A CMS rule (CMS-0057-F) set new requirements, generally beginning January 1, 2026, for Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid and CHIP (both fee-for-service and managed care), and Qualified Health Plans on the federal Marketplace. Exact compliance dates vary by type of plan.

  • A specific reason: these plans must give a specific reason when they deny a prior authorization request. A vague “not medically necessary” isn't the whole answer. Ask what was missing.
  • Faster decisions: Medicare Advantage, Medicaid and CHIP must decide within 72 hours for expedited (urgent) requests and 7 calendar days for standard requests. This time limit doesn't apply to Marketplace plans under this rule.

These rules don't apply to prescription drugs.

Job-based plans

A request for approval before a scan is a “pre-service claim” under federal claims rules for job-based plans. The plan must decide an urgent pre-service claim as soon as possible given your medical condition, and no later than 72 hours after it gets the claim. For a non-urgent pre-service claim, the deadline is generally 15 days, and an appeal of a denied pre-service claim is generally decided within 30 days. If the denial is based on medical judgment, the plan must consult a health care professional with appropriate training and experience in that field when it decides your appeal. You can also ask, free of charge, for the documents and other information relevant to your claim, including the guideline or criteria the plan relied on. How to appeal a job-based plan.

Marketplace plans and Medicaid

Marketplace plans must give you an internal appeal and, if they still say no, an independent external review. How to appeal a Marketplace plan. In Medicaid, the 2026 decision times above apply to both fee-for-service and managed care, and if a managed care plan upholds a denial, you can ask for a state fair hearing. How to appeal in Medicaid.

Emergencies

Marketplace and most job-based plans can't require prior authorization for emergency services. If your scan was done in the emergency department and denied for missing approval, say so in your appeal. More on emergency care and prior authorization.

How to get the scan approved

  1. Ask for the specific reason and the criteria. Plans often have a written policy for each type of scan. Get a copy.
  2. Ask your doctor's office to resubmit or appeal with the details the criteria ask for: your symptoms and how long you've had them, exam findings, treatments already tried (such as physical therapy or medication) and how they went, earlier imaging results, and any warning signs that make the scan urgent. What the doctor's letter should include.
  3. Ask about a peer-to-peer call. Your doctor can ask whether the plan offers a conversation with its reviewer.
  4. Ask for a fast decision if waiting could seriously harm your health. How expedited appeals work.
  5. Watch your deadline. Work out your appeal deadline.
  6. If the plan says no again, ask for independent external review. Find your state's rules.

Before you schedule a scan the plan hasn't approved, ask the imaging center what you'd owe if the plan doesn't pay.

Common questions

Does Original Medicare need prior authorization for an MRI?

In most cases, no. Medicare.gov says you usually don't need prior authorization for Original Medicare to cover services. Part B covers diagnostic tests like MRIs and CT scans when your provider orders them to find or treat a medical problem.

How long does a plan have to decide on a scan request?

Under a CMS rule that generally began January 1, 2026, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid and CHIP must decide prior authorization requests within 72 hours for urgent requests and 7 calendar days for standard ones.

Are MRI denials worth appealing?

Often. In Medicare Advantage in 2024, 80.7% of appealed prior authorization denials were partly or fully overturned, but only 11.5% of denials were appealed.
