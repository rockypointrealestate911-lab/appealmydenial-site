Fertility treatment denied

Reviewed November 23, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: There is no federal requirement for insurance to cover fertility treatment, so coverage depends on your plan and your state. Some states require certain plans to cover infertility care or IVF, but those laws don't apply to self-funded employer plans. Start by finding out whether the plan says the treatment isn't covered at all or that you didn't meet its criteria. Criteria denials, such as the plan's definition of infertility, a prior authorization or medical necessity, are often the ones you can challenge with your medical records.

Why coverage varies so much

  • No federal mandate. KFF says there is currently no federal requirement for insurance coverage of fertility services. Congress would need to pass a new law to require or subsidize fertility and IVF coverage.
  • Not one of the ten essential health benefit categories. Marketplace plans must cover ten broad categories of essential health benefits, and infertility treatment isn't one of them. But the specific services within those categories can vary based on your state's requirements. Some states have added infertility care to their benchmark. For example, CMS reported in 2024 that the District of Columbia expanded its benchmark to cover infertility treatment, including IVF and standard fertility preservation.
  • State laws. Some states require certain insurers to cover infertility treatment. KFF tracks these laws and notes they vary considerably: some apply only to certain kinds of policies, some name specific treatments such as IVF that must or must not be covered, and some only require insurers to offer coverage rather than include it.
  • Employer choice. Many employers offer fertility benefits even where no law requires it. KFF reports that about one in four (27%) employers with 200 or more workers offers IVF coverage, and about half (53%) of those with at least 5,000 workers.

Examples of state rules

  • California: large group health plans issued, amended or renewed on or after January 1, 2026 must cover the diagnosis and treatment of infertility and fertility services, including IVF. Small group plans must offer this coverage. Religious employers are excluded.
  • New York: large group policies (more than 100 employees) must cover three cycles of IVF. Age restrictions aren't permitted for infertility, IVF and fertility preservation coverage.
  • Texas: KFF lists Texas as a mandate to offer IVF coverage, with required coverage of fertility preservation for cancer-related (iatrogenic) infertility.

Rules change, and each state's law has its own limits. Check KFF's state table and your state insurance department.

Self-funded employer plans

State laws don't apply to self-funded employer plans, and KFF says most people with employer coverage are in self-insured plans. New York's insurance regulator puts it plainly: its protections don't apply if your employer self-funds the coverage. Your Summary Plan Description should say whether the plan is self-funded, or you can ask HR. If it is, the plan's own terms decide what's covered. How to appeal a job-based plan denial.

In October 2025, the federal agencies issued guidance explaining how employers can offer fertility benefits as a separate "excepted benefit" alongside their health plan. KFF notes there is no mandate for employers to offer these benefits.

Medicaid and Medicare

KFF says Medicaid rarely covers fertility services, and IVF is typically never covered by Medicaid or Medicare. Some state Medicaid programs cover limited services. For example, KFF lists New York Medicaid as covering infertility office visits, some diagnostic tests and fertility drugs. Medicare's benefit manual says reasonable and necessary services associated with treatment for infertility are covered under Medicare, though KFF notes IVF typically isn't.

Questions to ask before you start treatment

A call before treatment can prevent a denial later. Ask your plan, and write down who you spoke to and when:

  • Is my plan fully insured or self-funded, and which state is it issued in?
  • Which fertility services are covered: testing, medications, insemination, IVF, fertility preservation?
  • How does the plan define infertility, and what records do you need to see?
  • Which services need prior authorization, and who submits the request?
  • Are there cycle limits, dollar limits or a lifetime maximum, and how much have I used?
  • Do I need to use specific clinics or pharmacies?

Two kinds of denials

1. "Not a covered benefit." The plan excludes the treatment. You can still appeal, but you'll need to show the plan documents or a state law actually require coverage, for example that your plan is fully insured in a state with a mandate, or that the treatment falls under a benefit the plan does cover. How to answer a "not covered" denial.

2. "You don't meet our criteria." The plan covers fertility care but says you don't qualify yet. Common reasons:

  • The definition of infertility. New York, for example, defines infertility as failing to establish a clinical pregnancy after 12 months of regular, unprotected intercourse, or after 6 months for a woman 35 or older. Your plan's definition may differ, so check it, and make sure your records show the dates.
  • Prior authorization. In New York, for example, insurers may require preauthorization for infertility services, IVF and fertility preservation. What to do about a prior authorization denial.
  • Cycle or dollar limits. Plans may cap coverage. New York allows insurers to limit IVF coverage to three cycles. Ask how many cycles or how much of a lifetime maximum the plan has counted, and whether it counted correctly.
  • Medical necessity for a specific drug, procedure or number of embryos. How to appeal a medical necessity denial.

How to appeal

  1. Get the reason in writing and the exact plan language or clinical criteria used. How to read a denial letter.
  2. Find out what kind of plan you have: fully insured or self-funded, and which state it's issued in. This tells you whether a state mandate applies.
  3. Ask your fertility doctor for a letter that matches the plan's criteria point by point: diagnosis, how long you've been trying, tests done, treatments already tried, and why the requested treatment is the next appropriate step. What the letter should include.
  4. File the internal appeal within 180 days of the denial notice. For a treatment you haven't had yet, the plan must generally decide within 30 days. Work out your deadline · How to appeal step by step.
  5. Ask for external review if the plan says no again. Denials that involve medical judgment, or a finding that a treatment is experimental or investigational, can go to an independent reviewer, generally within 4 months of the final denial. The plan is required by law to accept the reviewer's decision. How external review works.
  6. Contact your state insurance department if you think a fully insured plan isn't following your state's law. Find your state's contacts.

If you're facing cancer treatment that could affect fertility, timing matters. Ask whether your plan or state covers fertility preservation, and ask for a fast decision if waiting would close the window. How to ask for an urgent appeal.

Common questions

Does insurance have to cover IVF?

There is no federal requirement. Some states require certain plans to cover infertility treatment or IVF, but those laws don't apply to self-funded employer plans.

Do state fertility mandates apply to my employer plan?

Only if the plan is fully insured. State laws don't apply to self-funded employer plans, which cover most people with job-based coverage.

Can I appeal a fertility treatment denial?

Yes. You can file an internal appeal within 180 days of the denial. If the denial involves medical judgment, you can then ask for an independent external review.

Does Medicaid cover IVF?

KFF says Medicaid rarely covers fertility services and IVF is typically never covered by Medicaid or Medicare. Some states cover limited services such as diagnostic tests.
