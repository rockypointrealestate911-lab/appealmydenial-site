Durable medical equipment denied

Reviewed November 16, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: Medicare Part B covers durable medical equipment (DME) when it's medically necessary, your doctor or treating provider orders it for use in your home, and you get it from a supplier enrolled in Medicare. Most DME denials come down to a missing or incomplete order or records, a supplier that isn't enrolled, or a missing prior authorization. Find out which, ask your doctor and supplier to fix the paperwork, and appeal within 120 days of your Medicare Summary Notice if needed.

What counts as durable medical equipment

Medicare defines DME as equipment that is durable (can withstand repeated use), used for a medical reason (typically only useful if you're sick or injured), used in your home, and expected to last at least 3 years. Examples Medicare lists include:

  • Manual wheelchairs and power mobility devices (power wheelchairs or scooters needed for use inside the home)
  • Hospital beds and walkers
  • Sleep apnea and Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) devices and accessories
  • Glucose monitors and supplies

Medicare says its list doesn't include all covered DME, so an item not named above may still be covered.

What Original Medicare requires

  • Medical need. Anyone with Part B can get DME as long as the equipment is medically necessary.
  • An order. Your doctor or treating provider must prescribe the type of equipment you need by filling out an order. Medicare may also require your doctor to provide more information documenting your medical need. If your needs or condition change, your doctor must complete a new, updated order.
  • An enrolled supplier. Medicare only covers DME from a supplier enrolled in Medicare. If your supplier doesn't have a Medicare supplier number, Medicare won't pay your claim. You can find enrolled suppliers at Medicare.gov/medical-equipment-suppliers.

What you pay: generally 20% of the Medicare-approved amount after the Part B deductible. Ask whether the supplier accepts assignment, meaning it agrees to accept the Medicare-approved amount as full payment, so you'll pay less out of pocket.

Why DME claims get denied

Read your Medicare Summary Notice or denial letter for the reason. Common problems include:

  • The order or records don't show medical need. The fix is usually more documentation from your doctor, not a different diagnosis. Medicare stopped using Certificates of Medical Necessity and DME Information Forms for claims with dates of service on or after January 1, 2023, because the same information is already on the claim or in your medical record. Your records need to show the medical need.
  • The supplier isn't enrolled in Medicare. Check before you order.
  • Prior authorization wasn't approved. Some items need approval first (see below).
  • The item doesn't fit Medicare's rules for that type of equipment, such as a power wheelchair needed only outside the home.

How to read a denial letter.

Power wheelchairs and scooters

Medicare has extra rules for power mobility:

  • Before you get a power wheelchair or scooter, you must have a face-to-face exam with your doctor. The doctor then submits a written order telling Medicare why you need the device and that you're able to operate it.
  • Your doctor must state that you need it because of your medical condition. Medicare won't cover a power wheelchair or scooter that you only need and use outside the home.
  • Certain power wheelchairs require prior authorization before Medicare will pay. Medicare may deny the request if it doesn't get all the information it needs, or if it finds you don't medically require a power wheelchair. If Medicare needs more information, your supplier may resubmit the request. If a claim is denied because prior authorization wasn't approved, you can appeal the claim decision.

CMS keeps a Required Prior Authorization List of DME and related items, which includes certain power mobility devices. Your supplier normally submits the request, so ask it whether your item is on the list and where the request stands.

CPAP and glucose monitors

CPAP: Medicare may cover a 12-week trial of CPAP therapy if you've been diagnosed with obstructive sleep apnea. After the trial, Medicare may continue to cover CPAP therapy if you meet with your doctor or other provider in person and they document in your medical record that you meet certain conditions and the therapy is helping. If continued coverage is denied, ask your supplier and doctor what records are missing.

Continuous glucose monitors: if you have diabetes, Medicare may cover a continuous glucose monitor and related supplies if your doctor confirms you take insulin or have a history of problems with low blood sugar, and that you (or a caregiver) have been trained to use the monitor. Ask your doctor to document this.

Renting, buying and repairs

  • Medicare pays for most DME on a rental basis. It buys only inexpensive or routinely bought items, like canes, walkers and blood sugar monitors.
  • For items like wheelchairs and hospital beds, Medicare pays rent for 13 months of continuous use; after the 13th month, the supplier must transfer ownership to you.
  • While equipment is rented, repairs and replacement parts are the supplier's responsibility. Once you own it, Medicare may cover repairs (80% of the approved amount, up to the cost of replacing the item), and may replace equipment that's lost, stolen, damaged beyond repair, or has reached its reasonable useful lifetime, generally 5 years from the date you start using it.

If the supplier says Medicare won't pay

A supplier may give you an Advance Beneficiary Notice of Noncoverage (ABN) before you get an item it thinks Medicare won't cover. An ABN isn't an official Medicare denial. If you want the item and want to keep your right to appeal, choose the option that has the claim sent to Medicare.

How to appeal in Original Medicare

  1. Find the denied item on your Medicare Summary Notice and note the reason.
  2. Call the supplier first. Ask what was missing. If it's a paperwork problem, the supplier and your doctor may be able to fix it.
  3. Get your doctor's support. Ask for an updated order and a letter explaining why you need the item at home, what you've tried, and what happens without it. What the letter should include.
  4. Ask for a redetermination within 120 days of receiving the notice. Work out your deadline · How to appeal in Original Medicare.

Medicare Advantage and other plans

Medicare Advantage plans must cover the same medically necessary categories of DME as Original Medicare, and must follow Medicare's coverage rules.If a plan denies coverage, you can appeal and get an independent review. How to appeal in Medicare Advantage.

Job-based, Marketplace and Medicaid plans set their own DME rules within federal and state law. Check your plan documents, get the reason in writing, and appeal. If the plan says no again, ask about independent external review. Find your state's contacts.

Common questions

Why was my Medicare wheelchair or other equipment denied?

Common reasons are an order or records that don't show medical need, a supplier that isn't enrolled in Medicare, a missing prior authorization, or an item that doesn't meet Medicare's rules for that equipment, such as a power wheelchair needed only outside the home.

How long do I have to appeal a Medicare DME denial?

In Original Medicare, ask for a redetermination within 120 days of receiving your Medicare Summary Notice.

Does Medicare rent or buy medical equipment?

Medicare pays for most DME on a rental basis. For items like wheelchairs and hospital beds, it rents for 13 months of continuous use, after which the supplier must transfer ownership to you.

Do Medicare Advantage plans have to cover DME?

Yes. They must cover the same medically necessary categories of DME as Original Medicare, and if a plan denies coverage, you can appeal.
