Denied because the claim was filed too late

Reviewed November 2, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: Reason code 29 means the claim was sent after the filing deadline. In most cases the provider files the claim, so the missed deadline is the provider's problem to sort out with the plan. Before paying anything, ask the provider why it was late and whether it can bill you. In Original Medicare the limit is 12 months from the date of service, there are a few exceptions, and the late-filing denial itself generally can't be appealed. If the provider never filed at all, you can file the claim yourself.

What the denial means

Claim Adjustment Reason Code 29 reads: “The time limit for filing has expired.” The plan isn't saying your care was unnecessary or uncovered. It's saying the claim arrived after the plan's deadline for submitting claims.

Look at the group code in front of it. The standard labels are CO, “Contractual Obligation,” and PR, “Patient Responsibility.” If you see CO-29, ask the provider's billing office directly why the amount is showing up on your bill.

Two different deadlines get mixed up, so keep them apart:

  • The filing deadline is for sending the claim in the first place. That's what code 29 is about.
  • The appeal deadline is for challenging a decision on a claim. Check that one here.

Original Medicare: the 12-month rule

For Medicare Part A and Part B, a claim must be filed no later than 1 calendar year after the date of service. If the last day falls on a federal non-workday, the deadline moves to the next workday. The rules allow more time in a few situations:

  • Medicare's own error. If the claim was late because of an error or misrepresentation by Medicare or an HHS employee or contractor, the deadline is extended through the last day of the 6th calendar month after the month you're told the error was corrected. This extension isn't available more than 4 years after the date of service.
  • Retroactive Medicare. If your Medicare coverage was approved retroactively to a date on or before the service, the deadline is extended through the 6th calendar month after the month you were notified.
  • Medicaid or a Medicare Advantage plan took its payment back. If, after retroactive Medicare entitlement or retroactive disenrollment from a Medicare Advantage or PACE plan, Medicaid or the plan recovered its payment from the provider 6 months or more after the service, the deadline is extended through the 6th calendar month after that recovery.

Can a Medicare provider bill you for a late claim?

CMS's claims manual (in a 2011 revision) says that when you asked for payment in time but the provider is responsible for not filing a timely claim, the provider may not charge you for the services, except for the deductible and coinsurance you would have owed if Medicare had paid.

The late-filing denial itself is not appealable: federal rules list a determination that a provider or supplier failed to submit a claim on time as an action that isn't an initial determination and can't be appealed. The same CMS manual adds that if you disagree that the claim was late, or with a finding that you are responsible for the late filing, the usual appeal rights are available to you. If you're being told you owe money because a claim was filed late, call 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227) or your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) and ask what applies to your claim.

If the provider never filed: file it yourself

Most Medicare providers file claims for you. If you paid and a provider refuses to file, Medicare.gov says you'll have to submit your own claim, and it must be filed no later than 12 months after the date of service. You'll need:

  • The Patient Request for Medical Payment form (CMS-1490S).
  • The itemized bill from the provider.
  • A letter explaining why you're filing yourself, for example that the provider refuses to file the claim.
  • Any supporting documents.

Mail it to the address for your state listed with the form. Don't wait: the 12-month clock still applies.

Medicaid

Federal rules require state Medicaid agencies to have providers submit all claims no later than 12 months from the date of service. Medicaid also limits participation to providers who accept, as payment in full, the amount Medicaid pays plus any deductible, coinsurance or copayment the program requires you to pay. If a Medicaid provider sends you a bill for a covered service, call your state Medicaid agency or your plan and ask whether you can be charged. How Medicaid appeals work.

Medicare Advantage

Medicare Advantage plans set filing and payment terms with their network providers in their contracts. Federal rules do require every Medicare Advantage plan to protect its members from being held liable for fees that are the plan's legal obligation to pay, and to make sure its provider contracts bar providers from holding members liable for those fees. If a network provider bills you after a late-filing denial, call the plan, explain what happened, and ask whether you can be billed. If the plan denies payment and you disagree, you can ask for a reconsideration. How Medicare Advantage appeals work.

Job-based and individual plans

Federal rules for job-based plans don't set a specific filing deadline, but the Department of Labor says a plan's claim procedures must be reasonable and can't unduly inhibit or hamper people from filing claims. Check your Summary Plan Description for the plan's filing limit, especially if you filed the claim yourself.

For provider-filed claims, many states set filing deadlines in law. Two examples:

  • New York requires providers to submit claims within 120 days after the date of service, unless the contract gives the provider more time. A participating (in-network) provider can ask the insurer to reconsider a claim denied only for being late if it shows the delay came from an unusual event and that it normally files on time; if the insurer pays, it can reduce the payment by up to 25%.
  • Texas applies a 95-day claim filing deadline to Texas-licensed physicians and providers, whether or not they're in network, though a provider and insurer can agree by contract to a longer period.

For a job-based plan, a denial of payment counts as an adverse benefit determination under the federal claims rules, so if you filed the claim yourself and think the plan applied the wrong deadline, you can appeal in writing. Your state insurance department can tell you what applies in your state. Find your state.

What to do, step by step

  1. Don't pay yet. Get the EOB and the provider's bill and check the group code (CO or PR) and the dates.
  2. Call the billing office. Ask: “When did you first submit this claim? Do you have proof it was sent on time? Under your contract with my plan, can you bill me for a late-filed claim?” If you gave them the right insurance information on time, say so.
  3. Look for a reason it was late. Wrong insurance on file, a coordination-of-benefits mix-up, or retroactive coverage can matter, especially for Medicare's exceptions above. Bring proof, such as the insurance card you gave at check-in.
  4. Call your plan. Ask what date the plan received the claim, what deadline it applied, and whether you can be billed for the amount.
  5. If the claim was never filed, ask the provider to file it now, or file it yourself if the plan allows it.
  6. Get help if you're stuck: 1-800-MEDICARE and SHIP for Medicare, your state Medicaid agency for Medicaid, and your state insurance department for private plans.

Common questions

What does denial code CO-29 mean?

CO-29 means the time limit for filing the claim has expired. CO stands for “Contractual Obligation,” so ask the provider's billing office why the amount appears on your bill before paying.

What is Medicare's timely filing limit?

Original Medicare claims must be filed no later than 1 calendar year (12 months) after the date of service. There are limited extensions, for example when Medicare's own error caused the delay or your Medicare coverage was approved retroactively.

Can I appeal a timely filing denial?

In Original Medicare, the late-filing denial itself isn't appealable, but CMS's manual says you can use the usual appeal rights if you disagree that the claim was late or that you're responsible for the late filing. For job-based plans, a denial of payment counts as an adverse benefit determination you can appeal in writing.
