Mental health or substance use treatment denied: how to use parity rules

Reviewed October 26, 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: Most job-based and individual health plans that cover mental health or substance use care can't make that care harder to get than comparable medical and surgical care. That covers copays and deductibles, visit and day limits, and rules like prior authorization and medical necessity criteria. Appeal the denial, and ask the plan in writing for the medical necessity criteria it used and its comparison of how those rules apply to medical care. Denials based on medical judgment can go to an independent external review.

What “parity” means

The federal Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act (MHPAEA) doesn't require a plan to cover mental health or substance use disorder care. But if a plan does cover it, the limits it puts on that care can't be more restrictive than the predominant limits it puts on medical and surgical care in the same category, such as inpatient, outpatient, emergency care or prescription drugs. Marketplace plans have to cover mental health and substance use services as essential health benefits.

Parity applies to three kinds of limits:

  • Money limits (financial requirements): deductibles, copayments, coinsurance and out-of-pocket maximums.
  • Number limits (quantitative treatment limitations): caps on the number of visits or days covered.
  • Rule-based limits (nonquantitative treatment limitations): things like prior authorization, medical necessity criteria, “fail first” rules and network standards. The plan's processes, evidence and standards for these have to be comparable to, and applied no more strictly than, those it uses for medical and surgical care.

That third group is where most real-world disputes happen. A plan can require prior authorization for therapy. What it can't do is use a stricter process for mental health care than it uses for comparable medical care.

Which plans have to follow parity

  • Most job-based plans, including self-funded plans of larger employers. Self-insured employer plans covering 50 or fewer employees are exempt.
  • Individual and small-group insurance, including Marketplace plans.
  • Medicaid managed care, CHIP, and Medicaid Alternative Benefit Plans. If you have Medicaid, use your state's process: how to appeal a Medicaid denial. These parity rules generally don't reach other Medicaid behavioral health services paid fee-for-service.
  • State and local government employer plans. Self-funded plans used to be able to opt out of parity, but new opt-outs ended in December 2022 and existing ones could not be renewed after June 2023 (some plans under union contracts can keep one until the last contract ends). CMS, not the Department of Labor, handles parity for these plans.

Other exceptions include retiree-only plans. Medicare is not covered by the parity law, either Original Medicare or Medicare Advantage. Medicare denials still have their own appeal rights: Medicare Advantage or Original Medicare.

Signs a denial might be a parity problem

Federal regulators publish a list of “warning signs”: plan rules that call for a closer look to see whether they meet parity. These are not automatic violations. They are signals that the plan should be able to show it treats medical care the same way. Examples from that list:

  • Requiring preauthorization for all mental health or substance use care.
  • Requiring that you first fail to make progress in less-intensive outpatient care before intensive outpatient treatment is covered.
  • Requiring a likelihood of improvement before covering residential treatment.
  • Requiring individual treatment plans to be submitted on a regular basis.
  • Excluding residential treatment for substance use disorders.
  • Geographic limits on mental health or substance use treatment that don't apply to medical care.
  • Requiring mental health or substance use facilities to be state-licensed when the plan doesn't require the same of medical facilities.

Ask yourself: would the plan apply the same rule to a comparable medical service, like a stay in a skilled nursing or rehab facility after surgery? If you can't tell, that's exactly what to ask the plan.

Your right to see the plan's rules

You don't have to guess. You have the right to ask for:

  • The medical necessity criteria for mental health and substance use benefits. The plan has to make them available on request to current or potential members and to contracting providers.
  • The reason for the denial, in writing.
  • Your claim documents, free of charge: all documents, records and other information relevant to your claim.
  • The plan's comparative analysis for job-based plans covered by ERISA: the plan's written comparison of how it designs and applies a rule like prior authorization to mental health care versus medical care. Members, and their authorized representatives, are entitled to it on request.

Put the request in writing, keep a copy, and note the date you sent it. You can use plain wording like this:

“I am requesting, free of charge: (1) the medical necessity criteria used to deny my claim for [service] on [date]; (2) all documents, records and other information relevant to this claim; and (3) the comparative analysis of the nonquantitative treatment limitations applied to this benefit, including how the plan applies the same type of limit to comparable medical/surgical benefits.”

How to appeal, step by step

  1. Find your deadline. For job-based and Marketplace plans you generally have at least 180 days from receiving the denial to file an internal appeal. Don't wait for the documents you requested to arrive before filing. Check your deadline.
  2. Decide if it's urgent. If you're in residential or inpatient treatment, or waiting could seriously harm your health, ask for an expedited appeal. Urgent appeals are generally decided within 72 hours.
  3. Answer the stated reason. Most mental health denials say the care is “not medically necessary” or isn't the right level of care. Ask your clinician for a letter that answers the plan's criteria point by point. What a strong medical necessity appeal includes.
  4. Raise parity in plain words. In your appeal letter, say you believe the denial may violate the Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act, name the rule you're challenging (for example, a progress requirement for intensive outpatient care), and ask the plan to show that it applies the same rule to comparable medical care.
  5. Keep records. Write down every call: date, name, and reference number.
  6. If the plan says no again, ask for external review. Denials that involve medical judgment, including medical necessity and level of care, can go to an independent external reviewer. You generally have 4 months after the final internal denial. Find your state's program.

Where to get help or complain

  • Job-based plans: the Department of Labor's Employee Benefits Security Administration (EBSA) has benefits advisors at 1-866-444-3272 or askebsa.dol.gov.
  • Insurance bought on your own, or a fully insured employer plan: your state insurance department generally has authority over the insurer. Find your state.
  • State and local government employer plans: CMS handles parity questions and complaints.

A note on the federal rules in 2026

New federal parity rules were issued in September 2024. On May 15, 2025, the Departments of Labor, Health and Human Services, and the Treasury said they would not enforce the parts of that rule that were new while a lawsuit over it is pending, plus 18 months. The Departments have also said they are reconsidering the 2024 rule, so its future is uncertain. The parity law itself, including the 2021 requirement that plans do comparative analyses, and the earlier 2013 rules are still in effect. Your rights described above come from the law and those earlier rules.

Common questions

Does parity mean my plan has to cover mental health care?

The federal parity law itself doesn't require a plan to cover mental health or substance use care. But if it does, the limits can't be stricter than those on comparable medical care. Marketplace plans must cover mental health and substance use services as essential health benefits.

Does the parity law apply to Medicare?

No. The federal parity law doesn't apply to Original Medicare or Medicare Advantage. You can still appeal a Medicare denial through Medicare's own appeal process.

How do I get the criteria my plan used to deny mental health treatment?

Ask in writing. Plans must make their medical necessity criteria for mental health and substance use benefits available on request, and must give you free copies of the documents relevant to your claim.
